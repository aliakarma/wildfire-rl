% ABSTRACT
% ====================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Coordinating multi-agent teams to defend critical infrastructure during wildfires couples strategic asset prioritization with local suppression. We contribute (i) an \emph{infrastructure-aware wildfire-coordination benchmark} built on the validated Cell2Fire physics engine and two documented, GIS-derived landscapes---Saudi Arabian petroleum infrastructure and California Wildland--Urban Interface (WUI) zones---under a suppression-relevant regime with a uniform 5-seed $\times$ 15-episode protocol; and (ii) \textbf{HierComm}, a \emph{value-aware hierarchical} multi-agent policy that composes a rule-based value-aware asset assignment with a learned tactical policy trained by imitation of a value-aware expert and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning. Because the strongest baseline differs by region, consistent performance across both landscapes is non-trivial. HierComm attains the best average Weighted Economic Loss and Infrastructure Survival Rate in both regions; these improvements are statistically significant against the communicating baseline and the heuristic teacher and are largest on the scattered-asset (California) landscape, while on the concentrated-asset (Saudi) landscape the margin over the strongest flat baseline is within seed variance. A component ablation attributes the gains to the value-aware hierarchy, the learned tactical policy, and RL fine-tuning, and reports that explicit inter-agent communication is not a statistically significant driver at the three-agent scale studied. HierComm is also robust---strongest across an easy/medium/hard difficulty sweep and retaining a protective margin over inaction under held-out ignitions, rotated wind and asset layouts, and, with degradation, cross-region transfer. We make no claim of novelty for the underlying reinforcement-learning or communication primitives; our contributions are the benchmark, a simple and strong integrated system, an honest component analysis, and a fully reproducible release (code, checkpoints, GIS pipeline, hashed manifest).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Wildfire suppression demands coordinated allocation of limited suppression resources across a dynamically evolving hazard landscape. When critical infrastructure---petroleum refineries, pipeline junctions, residential structures---lies in the path of an advancing fire front, the coordination challenge intensifies: agents must \emph{strategically prioritize} which assets to defend rather than simply minimizing total burned area. This infrastructure-defense framing distinguishes wildfire suppression from generic fire-containment problems and motivates the present work.

\paragraph{Coordination under partial observability.}
This prioritization is compounded by partial observability: agents with local, egocentric views must decide \emph{which} distant high-value assets to protect, not merely suppress the nearest flames. Such prioritization can be difficult for flat decentralized policies such as MAPPO \citep{yu2022mappo} and QMIX \citep{rashid2018qmix}, which tend to react to locally visible fire, and the benefit of explicit inter-agent communication varies with how assets are distributed in space. Hierarchical decision making offers a natural decomposition of the problem across timescales: a strategic layer assigns agents to high-value threatened assets on a slow timescale, while a tactical layer handles local suppression on a fast one. We build our method, \textbf{HierComm}, on this decomposition---a \emph{value-aware commander} that dispatches agents toward the assets most worth defending, and a learned tactical policy, refined by reinforcement learning, that carries out suppression.

\paragraph{A benchmark and a method, together.}
Meaningful progress on this problem requires a testbed rigorous enough to trust and a method strong enough to matter; we contribute both. Our \emph{infrastructure-aware coordination benchmark} combines physically grounded fire spread from the validated Cell2Fire engine, two documented GIS-derived landscapes with contrasting asset geometries (concentrated Saudi petroleum infrastructure and scattered California WUI zones), and a \emph{suppression-relevant} regime whose difficulty was calibrated using only a no-operation baseline and a fixed heuristic---independently of the proposed method---so that skilled coordination, rather than inaction or luck, determines outcomes. Because the relative ranking of baselines shifts across these two landscapes, the benchmark rewards methods that perform consistently across differing asset geometries. Under a uniform 5-seed protocol, HierComm achieves the best average infrastructure-protection outcomes in both regions (Section~\ref{sec:results}); a component ablation attributes these gains to the value-aware hierarchy, the learned tactical policy, and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning, and finds that the explicit communication in the architecture does not significantly change performance at the three-agent scale we study. We further stress-test the method across difficulty regimes, held-out conditions, and cross-region transfer, where it remains protective though with degradation across regions. We do not claim novelty for the underlying reinforcement-learning or communication mechanisms; rather, benchmark and method are complementary contributions---the benchmark makes coordination skill measurable, and HierComm is a simple, reproducible system that meets that bar while its ablation clarifies \emph{why} it works.

\paragraph{Contributions.} Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An \textbf{infrastructure-aware wildfire-coordination benchmark} on the validated Cell2Fire physics engine and two documented, GIS-derived landscapes (Saudi petroleum and California WUI), under a \emph{suppression-relevant} regime in which skilled coordination measurably protects infrastructure, with a released data pipeline and hashed manifests.
    \item \textbf{HierComm}, a \textbf{value-aware hierarchical} MARL system that \emph{composes} standard components---a rule-based value-aware asset assignment, a communicating tactical actor, behavior cloning from a value-aware expert, and PPO fine-tuning---rather than introducing a new learning algorithm. It attains the best average WEL and ISR in \emph{both} regions, with WEL improvements over the communicating baseline (CommNet) and the heuristic teacher that are statistically significant; its margin over the strongest flat baseline is significant on California but within seed variance on Saudi.
    \item A \textbf{component ablation} that attributes the gains: the hierarchy, the learned tactical policy, and RL fine-tuning each contribute significantly, whereas explicit inter-agent communication is \emph{not} a statistically significant driver at three agents---an honest account of \emph{where} the improvement originates rather than an assumed one.
    \item A \textbf{robustness and transfer analysis, with a fully reproducible evaluation protocol}: HierComm stays best across an easy/medium/hard difficulty sweep and keeps a positive protective margin over no-operation under held-out ignitions, rotated wind, rotated asset layouts, and cross-region transfer, characterized with a falsifiable within-condition $\Delta$WEL metric. All results follow a uniform protocol---5 training seeds, bootstrap 95\% CIs, Welch's $t$-tests with effect sizes---with released code, checkpoints, GIS pipeline, and a hashed reproducibility manifest.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Wildfire Simulation and GIS-Based Planning.}
Wildfire spread modeling has progressed from simple cellular automata to physics-based simulators. Cell2Fire \citep{pais2021cell2fire} implements the Canadian Forest Fire Behavior Prediction (FBP) system with heterogeneous fuel types, wind effects, and topographic slope corrections. Operational research approaches optimize firebreak placement using mixed-integer programming \citep{hu2009wildfire} but assume global state observability and static fire behavior, neither of which holds during active suppression. Our work uses Cell2Fire as a validated physics backend while framing suppression as a sequential decision problem under partial observability.

\paragraph{Reinforcement Learning for Wildfire and Disaster Response.}
Recent RL approaches to wildfire include UAV-based detection \citep{ghali2022, partheepan2023}, landscape-scale fuel-treatment planning \citep{altamimi2022wildfire}, and grid-based suppression agents on Cell2Fire \citep{shen2022firehose}. Most use simplified fire dynamics without validated physics, single-agent formulations, or toy grids that lack realistic infrastructure; the closest prior system, Firehose \citep{shen2022firehose}, is single-objective (burned-area) and single-agent. To our knowledge, no prior work targets \emph{multi-agent, infrastructure-weighted} suppression on a validated physics engine, which is the gap our benchmark addresses and on which we evaluate our proposed hierarchical method. Operations-research approaches remain the deployed state of the art for suppression \emph{planning}: they optimize firebreak or resource placement via mixed-integer programming \citep{hu2009wildfire} under global observability and quasi-static fire, and are complementary to---not competitors of---the sequential, partially observed, reactive-suppression setting we study. Disaster-response RL more broadly has explored resource allocation \citep{nguyen2019disaster} but rarely the spatial coordination of multi-agent infrastructure defense under physically grounded fire spread.

\paragraph{Cooperative MARL and Communication.}
Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) is the dominant paradigm for cooperative MARL. QMIX \citep{rashid2018qmix} factorizes joint value functions under monotonicity constraints; MAPPO \citep{yu2022mappo} applies PPO \citep{schulman2017} with centralized critics. CommNet \citep{sukhbaatar2016learning} and TarMAC \citep{das2019tarmac} add learned communication channels. On our benchmark the effectiveness of these decentralized and communication-based methods varies by region and neither dominates; our value-aware hierarchical method, which pairs asset-value--driven assignment with a learned tactical policy, outperforms both. An ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) further finds that explicit communication does not, on its own, provide a statistically significant benefit at the three-agent scale we study.

\paragraph{Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning.}
The options framework \citep{sutton1999options} and feudal networks \citep{vezhnevets2017feudal} introduce temporal abstraction via subgoal hierarchies. HAM \citep{parr1998hams} and MAXQ \citep{dietterich2000maxq} decompose MDPs into subtask hierarchies. In multi-agent settings, RODE \citep{wang2021rode} uses role decomposition, while MAVEN \citep{mahajan2019maven} conditions on shared latent variables. Most prior HRL work jointly optimizes a learned low-level policy with the high-level controller. Our approach pairs a \emph{value-aware} strategic assignment (each agent to a high-value threatened asset) with a \emph{learned} communicating tactical policy that is behavior-cloned from a value-aware asset-defense expert and then refined by RL; we attribute performance to each component through ablation.

\paragraph{Imitation Learning and RL Fine-Tuning.}
Combining imitation with RL is well-established: DAgger \citep{ross2011reduction} iteratively corrects distributional shift; behavioral cloning followed by RL fine-tuning has been applied to game playing \citep{silver2016mastering} and robotics \citep{rajeswaran2017learning}. KL-regularized policy optimization prevents catastrophic forgetting of imitation priors \citep{wu2019behavior}. We apply this paradigm to the \emph{tactical} layer of a hierarchical wildfire-coordination policy, using a value-aware asset-defense heuristic as the behavior-cloning teacher.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

We model wildfire suppression as a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP) extended with hierarchical temporal abstraction.

\paragraph{State.}
The global state $s = \langle S_{\text{fire}}, S_{\text{asset}}, S_{\text{agent}} \rangle$ consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $S_{\text{fire}} \in \{0,1\}^{H \times W}$: binary active-fire grid evolved by Cell2Fire physics.
    \item $S_{\text{asset}} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{H \times W}$: infrastructure criticality map encoding economic asset values.
    \item $S_{\text{agent}} = \{(y_i, x_i)\}_{i=1}^N$: grid positions of $N$ suppression agents.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Observations.}
Each agent $i$ observes an egocentric $C \times C$ crop ($C = 9$) containing 8 channels: active fire, treated cells, fuel type, criticality, agent occupancy, normalized coordinates, fire fraction, and time fraction. Agents do \emph{not} observe the full grid.

\paragraph{Actions.}
The problem is hierarchical in time. At the \emph{strategic} level, every $T_{\text{macro}} = 10$ steps each agent is assigned a target---a high-value threatened asset to defend. At the \emph{tactical} level, micro-actions $a_i^t \in \{$stay, up, down, left, right, treat$\}$ are executed at every environment step to reach and defend that target.

\paragraph{Reward.}
The suppression objective, used both to train and to evaluate, rewards protecting economic value:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
R = -\Delta \text{WEL} + \alpha \cdot \Delta \text{ISR}
\end{equation}
where $\text{WEL} = \sum_j v_j \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{asset}_j \text{ burned}]$ is the Weighted Economic Loss, $\text{ISR} = |\{\text{surviving assets}\}| / |\{\text{total assets}\}|$ is the Infrastructure Survival Rate, and $\alpha$ upweights survival (Appendix~A). This objective directly incentivizes infrastructure defense over generic burned-area minimization.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\textbf{HierComm} decomposes infrastructure defense into a value-aware strategic layer and a learned tactical layer (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). Every $T_{\text{macro}} = 10$ steps, a value-aware commander assigns each agent a high-value threatened asset to defend; between assignments, a learned communicating tactical policy selects micro-actions that build the firebreak protecting the assigned asset. The tactical policy is warm-started by behavior cloning and then refined end-to-end by reinforcement learning on the suppression objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}).

\subsection{Value-Aware Strategic Commander}
\label{sec:commander}
The commander assigns each agent a target asset by threat-weighted value. For every asset $j$ with economic value $v_j$, it computes a threat score $\tau_j = v_j / (1 + d_j)$, where $d_j$ is the distance from asset $j$ to the nearest active-fire cell; agents are assigned to the highest-threat assets (ranked by $\tau_j$, or by value when no fire is present). The assigned asset location becomes the agent's strategic target $(y_i^*, x_i^*)$, re-evaluated every $T_{\text{macro}}$ steps. Prioritizing valuable assets that are \emph{imminently threatened}---rather than merely nearby fire---is the strategic component of HierComm; we isolate its contribution by ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). We also implement a learned-commander variant (a network over coarse fire/asset/agent grids, warm-started and updated by policy gradient jointly with the tactical layer); \textbf{all HierComm results we report use the value-aware assignment above}, and the learned-commander variant is evaluated only in the ablation.

\subsection{Learned Communicating Tactical Policy}
\label{sec:tactical}
Given its assigned target, each agent selects micro-actions with a shared-parameter neural policy. Agent $i$ (i) encodes its egocentric $9{\times}9$, 8-channel observation with a small CNN into a feature $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$; (ii) encodes the relative direction to its target $(\Delta y_i, \Delta x_i) \in [-1,1]^2$ and fuses it with $h_i$; (iii) exchanges $R = 2$ rounds of mean-pooled messages with teammates,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comm}
h_i \leftarrow \tanh\!\bigl(W_h\, h_i + W_c\, \bar{m}_{-i}\bigr), \qquad \bar{m}_{-i} = \tfrac{1}{N-1}\!\sum_{k \neq i} h_k,
\end{equation}
and (iv) maps the result to masked logits over $\{$stay, up, down, left, right, treat$\}$. Parameters are shared across agents, so the policy is agent-count agnostic. The message-passing rounds in Eq.~\ref{eq:comm} are the explicit \emph{communication} component of the architecture; we measure their contribution directly in the ablation rather than assuming it.

\subsection{Training: BC Warm-Start then PPO}
\label{sec:training}
The suppression reward is sparse---economic loss registers only when fire reaches an asset, many steps after the actions that determined the outcome---so we train the tactical policy in two phases.

\paragraph{Phase 1: Behavior-cloning warm-start.}
We clone a value-aware \emph{asset-defense} expert: given an agent's assigned asset, the expert suppresses the fire-frontier cell nearest that asset---building the firebreak that protects it---and otherwise advances toward it. Cloning this value-aware defense, rather than value-blind nearest-fire suppression, initializes the tactical policy with asset-directed behavior. The tactical policy is trained by cross-entropy on the expert's micro-actions over $30$ demonstration episodes.

\paragraph{Phase 2: PPO fine-tuning.}
The warm-started policy is refined with PPO \citep{schulman2017} on the suppression objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}), using a centralized critic and generalized advantage estimation. A light firebreak-shaping term (coefficient $\eta$), proportional to the per-step reduction in actively burning cells, densifies the otherwise sparse signal. Training runs for $100$k environment steps per seed; the value-aware commander is fixed during tactical learning, so reinforcement learning refines tactical execution rather than re-deriving the assignment. Hyperparameters (communication rounds $R{=}2$, PPO clip $0.2$, GAE $\lambda{=}0.95$, entropy and shaping coefficients) match the released checkpoints (Appendix~A).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{HierComm Training}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Phase 1 (BC):} collect value-aware asset-defense demonstrations; train tactical policy $\pi_\phi$ by cross-entropy
\STATE Initialize centralized critic $V_\psi$
\FOR{iteration $= 1$ to $T$}
    \STATE Collect rollouts: every $T_{\text{macro}}$ steps, the value-aware commander assigns each agent a threat-weighted asset; $\pi_\phi$ acts with $R$ communication rounds
    \STATE $r_t \leftarrow -\Delta\text{WEL} + \alpha\,\Delta\text{ISR} + \eta\,(\text{burning}_{t-1} - \text{burning}_t)$
    \STATE Compute GAE advantages using $V_\psi$
    \STATE Update $\pi_\phi, V_\psi$ by clipped PPO with an entropy bonus
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Wildfire Simulator.}
We use Cell2Fire \citep{pais2021cell2fire}, a validated wildfire spread simulator implementing the Canadian FBP system. Fire evolves on $32 \times 32$ grids with heterogeneous fuel types, wind direction, and topographic slope effects.

\paragraph{Infrastructure Topographies.}
We evaluate on two real-world GIS-derived topographies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Saudi Arabia (Eastern Province)}: Petroleum refineries and pipeline junctions with high localized economic values.
    \item \textbf{California (WUI)}: Scattered residential structures typical of Wildland-Urban Interface zones.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Agents and Baselines.}
$N = 3$ suppression agents operate simultaneously. We compare:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No-Op}: agents take no suppression actions.
    \item \textbf{Value-First}: hand-crafted heuristic that assigns agents to high-value threatened assets; its assignment rule also drives HierComm's value-aware commander.
    \item \textbf{Greedy-Risk}: assigns agents to the highest fire-load sectors.
    \item \textbf{Local Reactive}: routes each agent to the nearest treatable fire-frontier cell.
    \item \textbf{Flat MARL (MAPPO)}: decentralized PPO agents without strategic coordination.
    \item \textbf{CommNet}: decentralized agents with learned inter-agent communication.
    \item \textbf{HierComm (ours)}: our value-aware hierarchical method with a learned tactical policy (Section~\ref{sec:method}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:training}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weighted Economic Loss (WEL $\downarrow$)}: Total value of burned infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure Survival Rate (ISR $\uparrow$)}: Fraction of assets unburned.
    \item \textbf{Coordination Efficiency (CE $\uparrow$)}: Fraction of unique cells treated (higher = less spatial overlap).
    \item \textbf{Total Reward ($\uparrow$)}: Cumulative shaped reward.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Statistical Protocol.}
Learned methods use \textbf{5 independent training seeds}; each checkpoint is evaluated on a held-out ignition stream (\textbf{15 episodes per seed group}), and the \emph{per-training-seed mean} is the unit of analysis ($n=5$). Heuristic policies are deterministic given the evaluation stream. We report means with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals and Welch's $t$-tests with effect sizes (Cohen's $d$, pooled SD; omitted where a distribution is degenerate). The learned methods---MAPPO, CommNet, and HierComm---are trained for $100$k environment steps on the same WEL/ISR objective used for evaluation (Appendix~A, with training curves in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}), removing any objective-level confound.

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} reports the multi-seed results. We report WEL and ISR, the infrastructure-relevant metrics; the shaped training reward is dominated by a policy-independent burned-area term and is not comparable across policies. Three findings emerge.

\paragraph{Finding 1: HierComm achieves the best infrastructure protection in both regions.}
HierComm attains the lowest WEL and highest ISR in both Saudi (WEL $7.02$, ISR $0.809$) and California (WEL $5.88$, ISR $0.832$). It improves significantly in WEL on every heuristic and on the learned communication baseline: versus CommNet, $p=0.048$ (Saudi) and $p=0.016$ (California); versus reactive coverage, $p=0.010$ (Saudi) and $p<0.001$ (California); and versus the Value-First heuristic, $p<0.001$ in both regions. In Saudi its margin over the strongest flat baseline (MAPPO, $9.77$) is \emph{not} statistically significant ($p=0.32$), though HierComm is better on the seed means; in California it significantly outperforms MAPPO ($p=0.002$). We therefore claim a decisive win on California and a best-on-average, statistically-tied-with-MAPPO result on Saudi. On the seed means, no baseline matches HierComm's combination of low loss and high survival across both regions. Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} illustrates the difference on a representative episode: HierComm contains the fire and defends its assets, while the baselines and inaction let it overrun the landscape.

\paragraph{Finding 2: The learned policy substantially outperforms its heuristic teacher.}
HierComm is warm-started from the Value-First heuristic and then refined by reinforcement learning. Rather than merely reproducing its teacher, the refined policy \emph{surpasses} it by a wide margin: WEL $7.02$ vs.\ $18.84$ on Saudi and $5.88$ vs.\ $28.40$ on California (both $p<0.001$, large effect sizes). The learned tactical layer and RL fine-tuning---not the heuristic dispatch alone---are responsible for this gain, which our component ablation isolates.

\paragraph{Finding 3: The benchmark remains diagnostic; HierComm generalizes across it.}
No single baseline is strongest in both regions: flat MARL (MAPPO) is the best baseline on concentrated Saudi assets (WEL $9.77$), whereas reactive coverage is best on scattered California assets ($10.23$); the learned communication baseline (CommNet) is competitive on Saudi ($12.44$) yet among the weakest learned methods on California ($19.58$). Concentrated petroleum assets and scattered WUI assets reward different coordination strategies, and a single-region evaluation would conflate them---the benchmark's central diagnostic property. HierComm is the only method best (on average) in \emph{both} regions, indicating that value-aware hierarchical control performs consistently across the two studied asset geometries rather than exploiting one; whether this consistency extends to further geometries is an open question (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Three components account for the gains.}
The \textbf{learned tactical policy} is the largest contributor: replacing it with deterministic target-seeking under the same value-aware commander raises WEL from $7.02$ to $18.76$ (Saudi) and $5.88$ to $13.76$ (California), roughly halving ISR. Removing the \textbf{value-aware hierarchy}---leaving a flat communicating policy (CommNet)---raises WEL to $12.44$ (Saudi, $p=0.048$) and $19.58$ (California, $p=0.016$). Removing \textbf{RL fine-tuning}---using the behavior-cloned policy without PPO---raises WEL to $11.33$ (Saudi, $p=0.011$) and $9.46$ (California, $p<0.001$). Each removal significantly worsens infrastructure protection in both regions, and each lowers ISR correspondingly.

\paragraph{Communication: an informative negative result.}
Removing \textbf{explicit communication} (setting the message-passing rounds to zero) produces no statistically significant change in WEL (Saudi $p=0.30$; California $p=0.12$); the small numerical differences fall within seed variance, so we treat communication as having \emph{no measurable effect} in either direction rather than helping or hurting. Removing the \textbf{firebreak-shaping} term is likewise not significant ($p=0.24$ Saudi, $p=0.06$ California). We report this plainly: in the evaluated three-agent benchmark, the value-aware hierarchy, learned tactical control, and RL refinement account for the observed gains, while explicit message passing provides no statistically significant additional benefit. One possible explanation---which our experiments do not directly test---is structural: with a value-aware commander already assigning complementary asset targets to only three agents, some of the coordination that message passing might supply may already be provided by the hierarchy. Communication remains part of the architecture; we simply do not claim it as a source of improvement.

The components that account for these gains are also what sustain HierComm's robustness (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}): because the value-aware commander re-derives asset targets from the current fire and asset maps, the learned tactical policy keeps defending the right assets when conditions shift.

\section{Robustness and Transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}

Strong average performance is only useful if it holds under conditions the policy was not trained on. We evaluate HierComm along three axes: difficulty regimes, held-out perturbations, and cross-region transfer. Held-out and transfer results use a falsifiable metric, $\Delta$WEL $=$ WEL(No-Op) $-$ WEL(policy) computed \emph{within} each condition, so a policy that adds no value scores $0$ and cannot hide behind a favorable baseline.

\paragraph{Difficulty regimes.}
We re-evaluate the frozen policies under easy/medium/hard regimes that vary fire weather and ignition proximity (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). No-Op WEL rises monotonically with difficulty in both regions, confirming the axis is meaningful, and HierComm attains the lowest WEL in every regime, degrading gracefully (Saudi $3.3\!\to\!7.0\!\to\!11.3$; California $0.4\!\to\!5.9\!\to\!17.7$) while remaining ahead of every baseline. \paragraph{Cross-region transfer.}
Transfer between the two regions is the hardest test, as the asset geometries differ sharply. When a policy trained on the other region is deployed here, HierComm still improves over No-Op ($\Delta$WEL $+10.2$ evaluated on Saudi, $+14.4$ on California), but with clear degradation: its Infrastructure Survival Rate falls from native $0.81$/$0.83$ to $0.55$ (California$\to$Saudi) and $0.38$ (Saudi$\to$California)---a Transfer-Robustness-Score (transferred/native ISR) of $0.66$ and $0.47$. The comparison with CommNet is instructive rather than uniformly favorable: CommNet transfers slightly better in one direction (California$\to$Saudi, TRS $0.96$) but collapses in the other (Saudi$\to$California, TRS $0.10$), whereas HierComm avoids catastrophic failure in either direction. HierComm thus offers more \emph{consistent} transfer, not uniformly higher transfer; cross-region generalization remains a limitation for all methods and a target for future work.

\paragraph{Practical implication.}
Across difficulty and held-out perturbations---and, to a lesser degree, cross-region deployment---HierComm never drops to the inaction baseline, a consistency that matters for infrastructure protection alongside peak performance.

% 10. DISCUSSION
% ====================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

HierComm composes established ingredients---a hand-designed value-aware assignment rule, a CommNet-style tactical actor, behavior cloning, and PPO---and we claim no algorithmic novelty in these parts; its value is that this simple composition is strong and reproducible, and that the ablation localizes \emph{why} it works (the value-aware hierarchy and the RL-refined tactical policy, not message passing). We thus regard the benchmark and this analysis as the primary contributions, with the method a transparent reference point. The outcome is region-dependent by design: on concentrated Saudi assets several baselines already reach relatively low loss, so the achievable separation is small and HierComm's margin over the strongest flat baseline is within seed variance, whereas the decisive, statistically significant separation appears on scattered California assets, where value-blind strategies fail to prioritize among dispersed targets. Because every learned method optimizes the identical WEL/ISR objective and the regime was calibrated independently of HierComm (Section~\ref{sec:intro}), the comparison isolates coordination quality rather than a tuned objective or environment; significance nonetheless rests on $n=5$ seed means, which we read conservatively.

% ====================================================================
% 11. LIMITATIONS
% ====================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We acknowledge several limitations:

\paragraph{Communication shows no measurable benefit at this scale.}
Our ablation finds that explicit inter-agent message passing does not significantly affect performance in the three-agent setting studied (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). Whether communication becomes beneficial at larger team sizes, with denser interactions, or under a different message design is an open question we do not resolve here.

\paragraph{Fixed strategic assignment.}
The deployed HierComm uses a value-aware assignment rule rather than a learned commander (the learned-commander variant did not improve results). A rule-based strategic layer is transparent but may limit adaptability in situations its heuristic does not anticipate; learning the strategic layer productively remains future work.

\paragraph{Reward--metric coupling and evaluation scope.}
The tactical policy is trained on the same WEL/ISR quantities used for evaluation; while all learned baselines share this objective (removing relative confound), absolute performance should be read with that coupling in mind. All results use a single simulator (Cell2Fire), a single month of weather, and asset-criticality rasters that are stylized rather than economically measured (Appendix~G); cross-simulator and multi-season validation remain open.

\paragraph{Two landscapes and small-sample statistics.}
Our conclusions rest on two hand-constructed regions and $n=5$ training seeds. Two geometries cannot establish generalization to arbitrary asset layouts---we accordingly scope claims to the studied geometries---and several tests rest on five seed means. We therefore report effect sizes alongside $p$-values and read marginal results (e.g., HierComm vs.\ the communicating baseline on Saudi, $p=0.048$) cautiously; the single largest ablation effect, removing the learned tactical layer, is a deterministic policy with no seed variance and is reported by magnitude rather than a test. Broader validation across more regions, seeds, and simulators is future work.

\paragraph{Scale and transfer.}
We evaluate primarily at $N=3$ agents on $32\times32$ grids. The tactical policy shares parameters across agents and can run at other team sizes, but we have not tuned or validated larger teams; larger grids ($64^2+$) require rebuilding the infrastructure rasters and retraining. Cross-region transfer degrades markedly relative to native performance for all methods (transfer-robustness $0.47$--$0.66$ for HierComm; Section~\ref{sec:transfer}): HierComm avoids the catastrophic one-directional collapse of the communicating baseline but is not a strong zero-shot transfer method, so a policy trained on one asset geometry should not be deployed on a very different one without adaptation.

\paragraph{Fire dynamics.}
Each agent step advances the simulator half a simulated hour and the fire evolves every step; nonetheless the episode horizon ($\sim75$ hours) captures a single fire's active phase rather than a multi-day campaign.

% ====================================================================
% 11. BROADER IMPACTS
% ====================================================================
\section{Broader Impacts}
\label{sec:impacts}

Wildfire suppression research has clear positive potential: effective coordination can save lives and protect critical infrastructure. However, we emphasize that our system operates on a \emph{simulated} environment and is not validated for real-world deployment. Autonomous wildfire suppression decisions could lead to negative outcomes if deployed without human oversight, particularly in scenarios involving civilian evacuation or resource triage. We advocate for AI-assisted decision support rather than fully autonomous suppression.

% ====================================================================
% 12. CONCLUSION
% ====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented an infrastructure-aware wildfire-coordination benchmark on validated Cell2Fire physics with two documented GIS landscapes, together with \textbf{HierComm}, a value-aware hierarchical system that pairs a rule-based asset-value assignment with a tactical policy learned by imitation and RL fine-tuning. Under a uniform, reproducible 5-seed protocol, HierComm attains the best average WEL and ISR in both regions---a decisive, statistically significant margin on the scattered-asset (California) landscape and a best-on-average result that is statistically tied with flat MAPPO on the concentrated-asset (Saudi) landscape---and remains protective across difficulty regimes, held-out perturbations, and, with degradation, cross-region transfer. We claim no novelty for the underlying primitives; our contributions are the benchmark, the integrated system, and an honest component analysis showing that the value-aware hierarchy and RL-refined tactical policy---not explicit communication at this scale---drive performance. We release the code, checkpoints, GIS pipeline, and hashed manifests to support future work on learned coordination for spatially critical infrastructure defense.

\subsection*{E. Simulator: Cell2Fire Physics vs.\ Wrapper}

To avoid overstating what is physically validated, we delineate exactly which components come from Cell2Fire and which are our custom wrapper.

\paragraph{Cell2Fire (validated physics).} The fire spread itself is computed by the Cell2Fire C++ engine \citep{pais2021cell2fire}, which implements the Canadian Forest Fire Behavior Prediction (FBP) System: FBP fuel types (mapped from remote-sensing NDVI; Appendix~G), wind- and slope-driven directional rate of spread, one fire period $=1$ simulated minute, and stochastic spread governed by the rate-of-spread coefficient of variation. We invoke it through an interactive stdin/stdout step protocol.

\paragraph{Custom wrapper (not part of Cell2Fire/FBP).} The following are our additions and should not be read as validated fire physics: (i) the \emph{suppression/treatment} action model; (ii) the infrastructure \emph{cascade} model (per-neighbor ignition probability $0.1$ when an asset cell burns) --- the ``cascade probability'' of Appendix~A is a wrapper parameter, not an FBP concept; (iii) the infrastructure-weighted reward (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}); (iv) the PettingZoo \texttt{ParallelEnv} multi-agent layer with action masking (agents cannot move off-grid or treat non-fuel cells). Each agent step advances the simulator by $30$ fire periods (half a simulated hour), so the fire evolves at every step.

\subsection*{G. Data Provenance}

The two regions are built from public remote-sensing sources over a June 2025 window; raw data is not redistributed but is downloaded from the providers and processed deterministically into $32\times32$ Cell2Fire landscapes (converter \texttt{wildfire\_marl.data.to\_cell2fire}; every landscape carries a \texttt{conversion\_report.json} binding it to its input hashes).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regions / ROIs:} Saudi Eastern Province (lon/lat $[45.5, 23.5, 50.5, 28.5]$, desert regime) and Northern California (lon/lat $[-124.5, 36.5, -119.0, 41.5]$, forest/mountain). CRS EPSG:4326, north-up.
    \item \textbf{Fuels:} MODIS NDVI $\rightarrow$ Canadian FBP fuel classes via fixed, region-independent thresholds (Forestry Canada Fire Danger Group 1992; \citealp{pais2021cell2fire}). A declared sensitivity parameter.
    \item \textbf{Weather:} ERA5 (Copernicus) June-2025 extracts $\rightarrow$ hourly \texttt{Weather.csv}; FWI codes via the standard CFFDRS equations. Wind direction is the meteorological from-direction.
    \item \textbf{Topography:} SRTM DEM (public domain) $\rightarrow$ per-cell slope percent and uphill azimuth in FBP convention.
    \item \textbf{Ignitions:} NASA FIRMS detections restricted to burnable cells, detection-count-weighted with disjoint train/eval seed streams.
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure criticality.} The asset-criticality raster is a \emph{stylized} layer (Gaussian value falloff around public infrastructure locations), not measured economic valuations; it is a declared modeling choice, and asset values are a sensitivity parameter rather than ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Resolution abstraction.} \texttt{Forest.asc} declares Cell2Fire's validated 100\,m operating cell size while spatial patterns derive from coarser ROI tiles; absolute geographic distance is not preserved.
    \item \textbf{Known data debt.} The raw California NDVI GeoTIFF was not archived, so California NDVI is reconstructed under a declared linear assumption; this is covered by the fuel-map sensitivity analysis and flagged for closure by re-download.
    \item \textbf{Release.} Code, configs, checkpoints (Appendix~D), and the generated landscapes are released with a SHA-256 manifest; a fixed snapshot may be archived on Zenodo with a DOI.
\end{itemize}
